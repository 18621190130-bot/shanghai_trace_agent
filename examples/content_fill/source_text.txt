Lu Xun Former Residence + p4
Lu Xun’s former residence is easy to miss if you think of Shanghai only through its famous landmarks. Tucked into an ordinary residential neighborhood in Hongkou, this modest lane house was where Lu Xun spent the last years of his life, from 1927 until his death in 1936. What makes the place important is not the grandeur of the building, but the contrast between its ordinary surroundings and the extraordinary cultural world that developed around it. Lu Xun was one of the most influential writers and thinkers of modern China, and his writing was deeply concerned with a country struggling with modernization, tradition, social inequality, and the question of what a new China should become. Standing here, you are not simply visiting the home of a famous writer; you are standing in the kind of everyday Shanghai neighborhood where some of those larger questions were being debated, written about, and lived. In this sense, Lu Xun’s Shanghai can be compared to the literary cities associated with writers such as James Joyce’s Dublin or George Orwell’s London, where a writer’s work became inseparable from the streets and social realities around them. But Lu Xun’s story has a distinctly Chinese dimension: his generation was experiencing an extraordinary transformation, and literature became one way of confronting that change. Look beyond the house itself and notice the scale of the neighborhood—the narrow lanes, closely packed residences, and traces of everyday urban life. The setting matters because it reminds us that Shanghai’s modern history was not created only in grand buildings or political institutions; it was also shaped inside ordinary homes and neighborhoods like this one. Lu Xun’s former residence is ultimately a small doorway into a much larger story: how ordinary Shanghai life became part of China’s search for a modern identity.
Long Museum West Bund + p5
The Long Museum West Bund is worth understanding not simply as a contemporary art museum, but as part of Shanghai’s transformation from an industrial waterfront into a new cultural district. Its architecture incorporates the structural logic of the former industrial landscape, while the museum itself has become a major home for contemporary art. This makes the building interesting even before you enter the galleries: you are standing in a place where Shanghai’s industrial past and its cultural present occupy the same landscape. For a visitor familiar with London’s Tate Modern or Paris’s Musée d’Orsay, the idea may feel familiar—old industrial or infrastructural spaces being given a new cultural life—but the West Bund tells a specifically Shanghai story. The city has repeatedly reinvented its relationship with the Huangpu River, moving from a working waterfront shaped by factories, warehouses and transportation toward a public waterfront shaped by museums, parks and cultural institutions. Inside the Long Museum, contemporary art gives you another way to read this transformation: not as a clean break between “old Shanghai” and “new Shanghai,” but as a city constantly reusing and reinterpreting what came before. When you visit, notice the relationship between the museum’s heavy architectural forms, the open riverfront and the artworks inside. The experience is less about seeing a single famous object and more about understanding how Shanghai is using culture to rethink its industrial landscape.
Xintiandi North Block — Shikumen Neighborhood + p7
Xintiandi is often introduced to visitors as a fashionable shopping and dining district, but its deeper significance lies in the type of neighborhood it preserves: the shikumen lilong, one of the most recognizable forms of historic Shanghai housing. Shikumen literally refers to the characteristic stone-framed entrance, but the real cultural importance lies in what happened behind those doors. These were dense urban neighborhoods where private family life, shared lanes, neighbors, commerce and everyday social interaction existed in very close proximity. You can think of the lilong as something between a European residential courtyard, a townhouse neighborhood and a traditional Chinese community, but it does not fit neatly into any of those categories. That hybrid quality is part of what makes Shanghai distinctive. Xintiandi also raises a more contemporary question: what happens when a living neighborhood becomes heritage? The area has been transformed into a highly curated cultural and commercial destination, allowing visitors to experience the architectural language of old Shanghai while also seeing how the city turns historical space into part of modern urban life. Look closely at the relationship between the houses, the narrow lanes and the new businesses occupying them. The interesting story is not simply “old buildings preserved,” but the continuing negotiation between memory, commercialization and everyday life. Xintiandi is therefore a useful introduction to Shanghai itself: a city that has always absorbed outside influences, adapted them locally, and turned different layers of history into something new.
Zhangyuan + p9
Zhangyuan is one of the places where you can see Shanghai becoming a modern city in real time. Dating back to the late nineteenth century, the area developed from a private garden into a major public gathering space and later became a historic residential community. Its mixture of shikumen houses, garden villas and other architectural forms reflects the unusually international character of modern Shanghai. Rather than thinking of Shanghai’s history as a simple transition from “Chinese” to “Western,” it is more useful to see Zhangyuan as evidence of constant cultural negotiation: Chinese residents, foreign residents, new forms of entertainment, commerce and architecture all existed within the same urban environment. In that sense, Zhangyuan can be compared to historic urban districts such as London’s Covent Garden or Paris’s Marais, where architecture records layers of changing social life, but Shanghai’s version is marked by a particularly intense encounter between Chinese traditions and global modernity. The site also carries another important story: historic neighborhoods are not frozen in time. Zhangyuan has gone through relocation, restoration and redevelopment, raising the question of how a city preserves history while continuing to change. When walking here, pay attention to the coexistence of different architectural languages and the spaces between them. Zhangyuan matters because it shows that Shanghai has long been a city built through cultural encounters, adaptation and reinvention.
The Bund — Chen Yi Square + p14
Standing at Chen Yi Square on the Bund, it is tempting to see Shanghai simply as a spectacular skyline: historic European-style buildings on one side of the Huangpu River and Pudong’s skyscrapers on the other. But the real story is more complicated. The Bund was shaped by Shanghai’s transformation into an international trading port in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, and its buildings reflect the financial and commercial power that developed around that transformation. For a foreign visitor, this is one of the best places to confront the complicated history behind Shanghai’s global identity. The architecture may remind you of European financial districts such as London or New York, yet the urban landscape here emerged from a very different historical encounter involving foreign concessions, Chinese commerce, colonialism, migration and international trade. Across the river, Pudong represents another chapter: the rapid economic transformation of China since the late twentieth century. The two skylines therefore form an unusual visual conversation between different periods of Shanghai’s history. Instead of simply photographing the buildings, look across the river and read the city as a timeline: the historic Bund, the river that made Shanghai a port, and the modern skyline that turned the city into a global financial and commercial center. The significance of the Bund is precisely this tension between Shanghai’s international past, its complicated colonial history, and its contemporary identity as a Chinese global city.
Yuyuan Garden + p15
Yuyuan Garden offers a very different image of Shanghai from the glass towers and wide boulevards that dominate the modern city. Built as a traditional Chinese garden during the Ming dynasty, it was designed around a very different idea of space: rather than showing everything at once, a Chinese garden creates a sequence of framed views, hidden passages, changing perspectives and carefully controlled contrasts between rock, water, plants and architecture. For visitors familiar with European gardens such as Versailles, the difference is revealing. A European formal garden often emphasizes symmetry, scale and a clear visual axis, while a classical Chinese garden is designed to make a relatively small space feel much larger and more unpredictable. Yuyuan therefore gives you an opportunity to understand an important part of Chinese aesthetics: beauty is often created through suggestion rather than direct display. Its location in the heart of the old city also makes the experience particularly Shanghai-like. Step outside the garden and you immediately encounter busy streets, traditional commerce, temples and modern tourism. The contrast between the carefully composed interior garden and the energetic city around it shows how Shanghai contains very different historical rhythms within a few minutes’ walk. When you visit, pay attention not only to individual buildings but to how each doorway, window and path controls what you see next. Yuyuan is less a collection of beautiful objects than a way of seeing space.
Lu Xun Park + p16
Lu Xun Park is important because it turns the story of Lu Xun from something contained inside a museum or former residence into part of the everyday life of Hongkou. The park contains Lu Xun’s tomb and museum, but its significance goes beyond literary commemoration. Lu Xun became one of the most influential voices of modern Chinese literature precisely because he used literature to question society, tradition and the mentality of his time. For a visitor from abroad, it can be useful to think of the park alongside literary memorial spaces such as Père Lachaise in Paris or writers’ neighborhoods in London, but the atmosphere here is different: this is not simply a place where a famous writer is remembered. It is also a living public park where local people walk, exercise, rest and spend time with family. That combination is important. In China, historical figures can remain part of everyday public space rather than existing only inside specialized museums. The park therefore allows you to see how literary memory becomes part of a city’s collective identity. Look at the relationship between the museum, the tomb and the ordinary life happening around them. The interesting question is not only “Who was Lu Xun?” but “Why does this city still choose to remember him here, in the middle of everyday urban life?”
Shanghai City God Temple + p20
Shanghai City God Temple offers a glimpse into a side of the city that can easily disappear behind its modern financial image. The temple is connected to traditional Chinese popular religion and the local belief in city gods who protect particular communities, but its importance also comes from the commercial life that developed around it. Historically, temples in Chinese cities were not isolated religious buildings; they could become gathering points for markets, food, entertainment and neighborhood life. This makes the area useful for understanding how religion and everyday commerce have often overlapped in Chinese urban culture. If you are familiar with European medieval cities, you might think of the way churches and market squares once formed the social center of a neighborhood, but the Chinese temple-market relationship developed through a different religious and social tradition. Today, the area is highly commercialized and crowded with visitors, yet the deeper story is still visible in the relationship between the temple, surrounding streets, traditional shops and local food culture. Rather than treating it simply as a tourist attraction, look at how religious symbolism, commerce, architecture and everyday consumption exist side by side. The City God Temple helps explain an older layer of Shanghai: a city whose urban life was shaped not only by trade and government, but also by belief, community and popular culture.
Memorial Residence of Soong Ching Ling + p21
Soong Ching Ling’s former residence is more than a preserved villa belonging to an important historical figure. It offers a way into the complicated story of China’s transition into the twentieth century through the life of a woman who was connected to some of the most important political changes of modern China. Soong Ching Ling was the wife of Sun Yat-sen, a central figure in the establishment of the Republic of China, and she later became an important public figure in the People’s Republic of China. Her life therefore crossed political systems that are often presented as separate chapters of Chinese history. The residence itself, with its garden and Western-style villa architecture, also reflects the international character of Shanghai’s elite urban culture in the early twentieth century. For foreign visitors, her story can be compared with figures such as Eleanor Roosevelt or other politically influential women whose public roles developed alongside major transformations in their countries, although Soong Ching Ling’s historical position was shaped by China’s particularly turbulent transition from empire to republic and then to a new political order. Walking through the residence, notice how personal spaces, political history and international influences overlap. The house makes history feel less abstract: major political transformations were experienced by real people through their homes, relationships and daily lives.
Memorial of the First National Congress of the Communist Party of China + p24
The First National Congress of the Communist Party of China took place in Shanghai in 1921, making this site important not only to Chinese political history but to understanding how the modern Chinese state came to be. For visitors unfamiliar with Chinese history, the significance can be difficult to grasp if the site is treated simply as a political memorial. A better way to understand it is to think about Shanghai as a meeting point for new ideas in the early twentieth century. The city was connected to international trade, modern education, publishing, political movements and new forms of social organization, creating an environment in which young intellectuals were exposed to ideas that were circulating far beyond China. The meeting itself was small, but its historical consequences were enormous. In comparison with famous political sites such as the Independence Hall in Philadelphia or the Palace of Westminster in London, this location is much more intimate, and that contrast is precisely what makes it striking: world-changing political movements can begin in surprisingly ordinary urban spaces. When you stand here, try to imagine Shanghai not as the global metropolis you see today, but as a rapidly changing city in which young people were debating what kind of future China should have. The importance of this place lies in that moment of uncertainty—and in how profoundly the decisions made during that period shaped China afterward.
1933 Old Millfun + p27
1933 Old Millfun is one of the clearest examples of how Shanghai turns its industrial past into contemporary urban culture. Originally built as a large slaughterhouse, its reinforced-concrete structure was designed around practical industrial functions rather than decoration. Today, the building is associated with creative industries, restaurants, cultural activities and photography, creating an unusual contrast between its original purpose and its present use. If you have visited places such as London’s Coal Drops Yard or New York’s Meatpacking District, you may recognize the broader pattern: former industrial spaces are increasingly being transformed into cultural and commercial destinations. But 1933 tells a particularly Shanghai story because the city’s rapid modernization created enormous industrial infrastructure, much of which later became surrounded by dense urban neighborhoods. Instead of demolishing every trace of that industrial era, Shanghai has increasingly experimented with giving these structures new lives. Inside the building, pay attention to the ramps, bridges, columns and unusual circulation spaces. They were designed for industrial movement, but today they create an architectural experience for people. That transformation is the cultural bridge: a building once designed to move animals through a production system has become a place where people move through art, food, commerce and leisure. It shows how Shanghai continually turns yesterday’s infrastructure into tomorrow’s urban culture.
West Bund Museum + p28
West Bund Museum represents a newer chapter in Shanghai’s relationship with art and the outside world. Located along the transformed Huangpu River waterfront, the museum connects Shanghai with international contemporary art while also participating in the city’s broader effort to turn former industrial land into public cultural space. For visitors, it can be useful to think of the museum as part of a larger global movement in which cities use museums not simply to display art but to reshape how people experience their waterfronts and urban neighborhoods. But Shanghai’s story is particularly interesting because the West Bund was once strongly associated with industry, transportation and working infrastructure. Today, museums, galleries, parks and public spaces have created a completely different relationship between the city and the river. The museum therefore works on two levels: inside, it gives visitors access to contemporary art and international cultural conversations; outside, the surrounding waterfront shows how Shanghai is redefining itself as a cultural city. Rather than rushing through the galleries, notice how the building, the artworks and the riverfront fit together. The important question is not only what art Shanghai is displaying, but what kind of city Shanghai is trying to become through art.
Tank Shanghai + p29
Tank Shanghai is a particularly strong example of Shanghai’s ability to reuse industrial infrastructure without completely erasing its past. The art center was developed from former aviation fuel tanks, structures that once belonged to the city’s industrial and infrastructural landscape. Today, the tanks and surrounding spaces have been transformed into a contemporary art destination, creating a setting where industrial architecture and experimental culture exist together. For foreign visitors, this can be compared to the reuse of power stations, factories and warehouses as cultural institutions in cities such as London, Berlin or New York. Yet the experience here has a distinctly Shanghai character because the transformation is closely tied to the redevelopment of the West Bund and the city’s changing relationship with its industrial waterfront. The tanks themselves are worth looking at as much as the exhibitions: their enormous scale, curved surfaces and traces of industrial function make the building feel different from a conventional museum. What you are seeing is not simply an art center placed on an old site; the old infrastructure has become part of the cultural experience. Tank Shanghai therefore offers a useful way to understand contemporary Shanghai as a city that does not always choose between preservation and modernization—it often tries to make the two coexist.
Gate M West Bund Dream Center + p30
Gate M West Bund Dream Center represents a more contemporary side of Shanghai: a city where culture, leisure, retail and public space increasingly overlap. Located within the rapidly transforming West Bund area, the project is part of a larger shift from an industrial waterfront toward a mixed-use cultural and lifestyle district. For a visitor, its importance is less about one historic building or monument and more about observing how younger generations experience the city today. In many global cities, former industrial waterfronts have been transformed into spaces combining cafés, design, entertainment, public events and creative industries. The West Bund follows this broader international pattern, but with Shanghai’s particular scale and relationship to the Huangpu River. This is therefore a useful stop for understanding that Shanghai’s culture is not only found in old neighborhoods and historic monuments; it is also being produced through new forms of public life. Look at how people use the space rather than only what the buildings contain: where they meet, sit, eat, take photographs, attend events or simply spend time. The cultural value of the place lies in this everyday behavior. It shows how a former industrial landscape is becoming part of the social life of a new generation.
Star Art Museum+ p31
Star Art Museum is best understood through Shanghai’s long relationship with contemporary art and its position as a city where different artistic traditions and global influences meet. Rather than treating the museum simply as a place to see individual artworks, think of it as part of a larger cultural ecosystem in which galleries, artists, collectors, creative spaces and audiences interact. Shanghai has repeatedly reinvented its cultural identity through encounters between local traditions and international influences, and contemporary art provides one of the clearest places to see this process happening today. For visitors familiar with the gallery districts of London, Berlin or New York, the basic experience may feel familiar, but the questions being explored through contemporary Chinese art often emerge from a very different social context: rapid urbanization, changing identities, technology, tradition and the tension between local experience and globalization. When visiting, try not to ask only “What does this artwork mean?” Instead, ask what kind of Shanghai or China made this artwork possible. The museum becomes more interesting when you see contemporary art not as something imported from elsewhere, but as one of the ways Shanghai is actively negotiating its place in the contemporary world.
St. Ignatius Cathedral of Shanghai — Xujiahui Catholic Church + p32
Xujiahui Catholic Church is one of the most striking reminders that Shanghai has long been a meeting point between different cultural and religious traditions. Its Gothic Revival architecture immediately looks different from the traditional Chinese buildings around the city, but that difference is precisely the point: Christianity arrived in China through centuries of exchange, adaptation and sometimes tension between Chinese society and the outside world. The church also belongs to the history of Xujiahui, an area that became an important center of Catholic education, publishing and cultural exchange. For a European visitor, the architecture may feel familiar, perhaps reminiscent of churches in France or Italy, but the meaning changes when the same architectural language appears in Shanghai. It becomes evidence of how foreign religious traditions were introduced into, negotiated with and eventually woven into the fabric of a Chinese metropolis. Rather than seeing the church as a piece of Europe transplanted into Shanghai, look at the surrounding neighborhood and consider how different cultural systems have coexisted here. The real cultural bridge is the contrast between a familiar architectural form and its unfamiliar social setting. This church helps reveal Shanghai as a city shaped not by one cultural tradition, but by encounters between many of them.
Nanjing Road Pedestrian Street + p34
Nanjing Road is often introduced as Shanghai’s main shopping street, but its deeper significance is that it has long represented the city’s relationship with modern commerce and mass urban life. Walking here, you are experiencing a form of city space that would be familiar to visitors from New York’s Times Square, London’s Oxford Street or Tokyo’s Shibuya: crowds, advertisements, shops, lights and constant movement. Yet Nanjing Road developed within a very different historical context and became one of the most important commercial streets in China as Shanghai transformed into a modern metropolis. Its importance today is therefore not simply that it has many famous shops. It is a place where you can observe how millions of people experience the city through consumption, entertainment and public space. Notice the mixture of long-established brands, international businesses, Chinese retail culture and ordinary everyday shoppers. The street is also useful for understanding the speed of Shanghai. Historic buildings, neon signs, luxury stores, street vendors, department stores and new consumer experiences can coexist within the same few blocks. If the Bund shows Shanghai as a global city from the outside, Nanjing Road lets you experience that identity from street level.
Shanghai Museum of Arts and Crafts + pz_17d41390cac9
The Shanghai Museum of Arts and Crafts offers a different way to understand Chinese culture: not through monumental history, but through the skills of making. Housed in a historic European-style villa, the museum brings together traditional Chinese arts and crafts within a building that itself reflects Shanghai’s international architectural history. That combination is particularly revealing. Shanghai is often described as a city where East meets West, but the relationship is more complicated than simply placing two cultures side by side. Traditional Chinese crafts continued to evolve within a city that was rapidly absorbing foreign architecture, technology, fashion and commercial culture. For a foreign visitor, the museum can therefore be understood as a place where cultural continuity becomes visible through objects made by hand. Unlike a painting that can be understood primarily through looking, crafts such as embroidery, carving, weaving and decorative arts also carry knowledge about materials, techniques and generations of practice. Pay attention to the amount of time and skill contained in each object, and then look at the villa around you. The contrast is meaningful: a distinctly Chinese tradition of craftsmanship preserved inside a building shaped by Shanghai’s cosmopolitan past. Together, they tell a story about how the city has absorbed outside influences without simply abandoning its own cultural traditions.

Look at the old buildings, small lanes and changing streetscape, and imagine the conversations, publications and arguments that once moved through this neighborhood. Duolun Road matters because it shows how a relatively ordinary street can become part of a country’s cultural memory.
Fuxing Park + pz_bd40d04c7f5d
Fuxing Park is one of the best places to understand Shanghai through everyday public life rather than famous attractions. Designed during the period when Shanghai was developing as an international city, the park carries traces of European garden design while functioning today as a distinctly Chinese public space. That combination is more revealing than the architecture alone. In Shanghai, public parks became places where different forms of urban life could meet: people could walk, exercise, socialize, play games, dance, practice tai chi or simply sit and watch the city. For a visitor from Europe, the park may initially feel familiar because of its formal landscaping and tree-lined paths, but spend a little time observing how people actually use it. You may see older residents dancing in groups, people practicing traditional exercises, families gathering, or friends simply sitting together. These activities reveal something about Chinese urban culture that a museum cannot easily explain: public space is not just scenery, but part of social life. Fuxing Park therefore provides a useful counterpoint to Shanghai’s famous commercial streets. Instead of asking what Shanghai sells or what Shanghai builds, the park lets you ask a quieter question: how do Shanghai’s residents actually spend their time together?

Wukang Road is one of the clearest examples of why Shanghai’s cultural identity cannot be reduced to individual monuments. You can compare it with historic neighborhoods such as Montmartre in Paris or Notting Hill in London, where architecture, cultural memory and contemporary lifestyle overlap, but Wukang Road carries Shanghai’s particular history of Chinese and international influences. Look at the buildings, but also look at the trees, sidewalks, storefronts and people around them. The street shows how heritage can survive not by becoming frozen in the past, but by remaining useful and attractive in the present. Wukang Road is ultimately less about one famous building than about the feeling of walking through a city where several generations of urban life remain visible at the same time.
People’s Square + pz_0db17d82e9ec
People’s Square is one of the most useful places for understanding how Shanghai has changed politically, socially and spatially. Today it is surrounded by major cultural institutions, government buildings, transport connections and commercial districts, but the space has gone through very different identities over time. What makes it interesting is precisely this ability to change with the city. Rather than being a historic monument preserved in one fixed form, People’s Square reflects different stages of Shanghai’s modern development and the changing relationship between public space, government, culture and everyday life. For a foreign visitor, it may be helpful to compare it with Trafalgar Square in London or Place de la République in Paris—large urban spaces that become symbols of the city and places where public life intersects with political and cultural institutions. But People’s Square has its own Chinese urban story. Around one space you can encounter the Shanghai Museum, Shanghai Urban Planning Exhibition Center, theaters, government buildings, subway passengers, office workers and ordinary residents. Look at the square not as an empty plaza but as a crossroads of different forms of city life. Its significance comes from the fact that Shanghai’s history is not kept somewhere outside the present; it continues to happen here, every day, in the movement of millions of people through the city.
